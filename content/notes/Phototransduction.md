There are two types of photo receptors: rods and cones. They are like pixels.

Rods - Low light levels.
Cones - Higher light levels and detect colours.

The eye has a remarkable dynamic range (lots of light levels). There are 20 rods per cone.

The retina can detect differences in the intensity of light falling different parts of it.

How do photoreceptors work? Electromagnetic -> Chemical -> Electrical energy

This is called Phototransduction. As photons fall onto the retina, they are absorbed by the photoreceptor cells in the outer segment and transduced into chemical energy. This chemical energy causes a change in the membrane potential in the inner segment, converting it into an electrical signal. This changes back to a chemical signal at the synaptic terminal.

## Rods

Transduction occurs in rods with the help of a membrane protein called Rhodopsin that is localised to the disks in the outer segment of the photoreceptor. It is composed of a G-protein coupled receptor (Opsin) and its ligand retinal (retinaldehyde or vitamin A aldehyde).

Like any GPCR, the binding of a ligand to its receptor activates the G-proteins in the membrane, which in turn stimulates the down-stream enzymatic generation of cytoplasmic second messenger molecules. These second messenger molecules can directly or indirectly change the conductance of ion channels, with the effect of changing the neuronal membrane potential.

Rhodopsins are different from other GPCRs because its ligand retinal is already tightly bound to the GPCR protein backbone opsin. When retinal absorbs a photon, the shape changes from a 11-cis retinal to all-trans retinal, which is the only light depended step.

Changes in the opsin backbone cause activation of the G-protein called Transducin, which in turn causes changes in the cytoplasmic concentration of second messenger molecules, cyclic GMP, by the action of an enzyme called cGMP Phosphodiesterase, which lead to the modulation of cGMP-gated ion channels and changes in the membrane potential.

Dark - Opens Na+ and Ca+2 ion channels, depolarising the photoreceptor after Na+ influx -> release neurotransmitter
Light - Deplets cyclic GMP in hte outer segment, closes ion channels and hyperpolarises the cell -> stop releasing neurotransmitter

One photon is enough to convert a 11-cis retinal to all-trans retinal, to initiate the phototransduction process. The functional consequences of a biochemical cascade for signal transduction is amplification. Each activated rhodopsin can activate hundreds of G-proteins, which in turn can activate many phosphodiesterases, which in turn generates thousands of cGMP molecules.
