For relativistic species, the #yield has no dependence on $$x_f$$. Neutrinos are a paradigmatic example and we must consider their contribution to the total amount of dark matter. Since they decouple while they are still relativistic, their yield reads $$Y_{eq}\approx0.278\frac{g_{eff}}{g_{*s}}$$.

Neutrinos decouple at a few MeV, when the species that were still relativistic are $$e^\pm, \;\gamma,\;\nu$$ and $$\bar{\nu}$$. Thus, the number of relativistic degrees of freedom is $$g_*=g_{*s}=10.75$$. For one neutrino family, the effective number of degrees of freedom is $$g_{eff}=3g/4=3/2$$. Using these values, the relic density today can be written as

$$\Omega h^2=\frac{\sum_i m_{\nu_i}Y_\infty s_0 h^2}{\rho_c}\approx\frac{\sum_i m_{\nu_i}}{91\;eV}$$.

In order for neutrinos to be the bulk of dark matter, $$\sum_i m_{\nu_i}\approx91\;eV$$, which is much bigger than the current upper limits (obtained from cosmological observations). Even if we consider the current bound $$\sum_i m_{\nu_i}\le0.3\;eV$$, it results in neutrinos contributing to 3% of the total dark matter density.
